# Research Project: Synthesis of the Archazolid Natural Products

## Project overview

The **archazolids** are a family of natural products — large, complex
macrolide molecules — first isolated from a soil-dwelling myxobacterium.
They are interesting because they are potent inhibitors of the **vacuolar-type
ATPase (V-ATPase)**, a proton pump that tumor cells rely on, which makes them
promising leads for new anticancer agents. The catch is supply: the bacteria
make only tiny amounts, and isolating useful quantities from nature is slow
and laborious. That is the central motivation for this project — if chemists
can **build the archazolids in the lab**, the molecules become available in
quantity for biology and drug development.

You will not make the entire molecule in one project. The archazolid skeleton
is far too large for that. Instead, like real total-synthesis groups, you will
focus on **preparing one fragment** of the molecule — a defined piece of the
overall structure that, in a full campaign, would later be joined to other
fragments to assemble the natural product. Your fragment is a real synthetic
target with its own short route of reactions to run, purify, and characterize.

This is a hands-on **wet-lab synthetic organic chemistry** project. It is
designed for a student who has completed introductory organic chemistry and is
ready to work at the bench. You are **not** expected to be an expert in
total synthesis or air-free technique at the start. By the end, you should
understand why natural products are made fragment-by-fragment, be competent
with Schlenk-line technique, and be able to read an NMR spectrum well enough to
say whether a reaction worked.

---

## The scientific question

Total synthesis answers a deceptively simple question: **can we make this
molecule, reliably and in useful amounts?** For the archazolids the answer is
"not easily yet" — the published routes are long and the molecule has sensitive
features (a delicate conjugated polyene region, several stereocenters) that
make some steps fail or scramble. Progress comes from solving the molecule one
fragment at a time.

Your piece of that question is concrete: **can you reproduce a route to an
assigned archazolid fragment, get it clean, and prove you made the right thing?**
The reactions involved are air- and moisture-sensitive, so the practical
sub-question is just as real: **can you run those reactions correctly under an
inert atmosphere, and can you use NMR to tell whether each step succeeded?**

---

## What you will actually do

1. **Learn the target and the strategy.** With your PI, settle on which
   fragment you will make and study its position in the whole molecule. Be able
   to explain why the synthesis is broken into fragments and where yours fits.

2. **Plan the route.** Work through the assigned sequence of reactions, step by
   step. For each step, know what bond is being formed or what group is being
   transformed, what reagent does it, and why the step is air- or
   moisture-sensitive.

3. **Run the reactions using Schlenk technique.** Set up and use a Schlenk line
   to exclude air and water — degassing solvents, working under nitrogen or
   argon, transferring reagents by syringe or cannula. Most of the key steps
   only work if oxygen and water are kept out.

4. **Monitor and work up each reaction.** Follow reactions by TLC and NMR,
   then quench, extract, and purify the product (typically by column
   chromatography).

5. **Characterize by NMR.** Acquire and interpret ¹H (and as needed ¹³C and 2D)
   NMR spectra of your intermediates and final fragment. Use the spectra to
   confirm structure and stereochemistry and to decide whether a step worked or
   needs to be repeated or optimized.

6. **Document everything.** Keep a lab notebook good enough that someone else
   could repeat your work: conditions, equivalents, yields, and the spectral
   evidence behind each conclusion.

---

## Required products

By the end of the project, you should produce:

- **An isolated, purified sample of your assigned fragment** (and the key
  intermediates en route to it).
- **Annotated NMR spectra** for the final fragment and important
  intermediates, with the diagnostic peaks assigned and explained.
- **A documented synthetic route** — the sequence of steps you ran, with
  reagents, conditions, and yields, presented so it could be reproduced.
- **A complete, organized lab notebook** capturing each experiment.
- **A short written report** that explains what the archazolids are and why
  they matter, describes the fragment you targeted and the strategy, presents
  your NMR evidence, and honestly discusses what worked, what didn't, and why.

## Suggested workflow

### Step 1: Build the background

Before touching glassware, make sure you can explain, in your own words: what a
natural product is, what a macrolide is, what V-ATPase is and why inhibiting it
is interesting for cancer, and why the archazolids' scarcity motivates a
synthesis. Then learn the convergent fragment-coupling logic of total synthesis
— why chemists make several pieces separately and join them late.

### Step 2: Lock down your fragment and route

With your PI, choose the fragment and write out its full reaction sequence.
For each step, identify the transformation, the reagent, the likely failure
modes, and which steps demand strict air-free conditions.

### Step 3: Learn the techniques on the bench

Get checked out on Schlenk-line operation and on the NMR instrument before you
rely on them for real material. Practice degassing, inert-atmosphere transfers,
and acquiring a clean ¹H spectrum.

### Step 4: Run the synthesis, one step at a time

Work through the route. After each step, use TLC and NMR to decide whether to
proceed, repeat, or optimize. Treat a "failed" step as data — figure out *why*
it failed before changing the conditions.

### Step 5: Purify and fully characterize the fragment

Purify your final fragment and acquire the NMR data needed to prove its
identity and stereochemistry. Compare your spectra against literature data or
predicted shifts where available.

### Step 6: Write it up

Assemble the route, yields, and annotated spectra into a clear report, and
explain what your fragment contributes toward a synthesis of the archazolids.

## Stretch goals

- **Optimize a step.** Take the lowest-yielding or least-reproducible step in
  your route and try to improve it, documenting a small condition screen.
- **Push toward a coupling.** If your fragment is finished early, prepare it for
  the union with a neighboring fragment (for example, installing the handle
  needed for a cross-coupling) and discuss the challenges of that junction.
- **Deeper NMR.** Use 2D NMR (COSY, HSQC, NOESY) to make a full, rigorous
  structural and stereochemical assignment of your fragment.

---

## What a successful project should demonstrate

A successful project does not require finishing the natural product — that is
the work of many people over many years. It should show clean, well-documented
fragment chemistry and a real command of the techniques. By the end, you should
be able to say:

- I can explain what the archazolids are and why a lab synthesis matters.
- I understand why complex molecules are built fragment-by-fragment.
- I can run air- and moisture-sensitive reactions correctly with Schlenk
  technique.
- I prepared and purified an assigned fragment of the archazolid skeleton.
- I can use NMR to determine whether a reaction worked and to confirm a
  structure.
- I kept records good enough that another chemist could reproduce my route.
